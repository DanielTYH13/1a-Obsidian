*Key Concepts:*
___

There are certain rules for conductors in equilibrium, the key being that 

For any ideal conductor with trapped charges, there exists a unique equilibrium distribution of charge regardless of external field. 

---
$E_{internal}=0$

In equilibrium, the charges within the conductor necessarily must not move. At the surface, this is restricted by the ideal conductor assumptions. For internal charges, this implies that $E_{internal}=0$.

---
$Q_{internal} = 0$

This can be shown using Gauss's law. Given a guassian surface within the conductor, the E field passing through the surface must be 0. Then,

$\Phi = \frac{Q_{e}}{epsilon} = 0$

And thus there cannot be any charge within the conductor. Ie, there are only chargest on the surface which create the field. 

---
*All components of the E field on the conductors surface are normal to the surface.* 

This is a given from equilibrium, and from the fact that all charge in a conductor is on the surface. 

Assume there is a net tangential component. In that case, there is some force and thus acceleration on the electrons beside it. Then, the system is not in equilibrium. This is a contradiction.

Field lines indicate this by twisting until they are normal to the surface.

---
$E_{applied} = E_{induced}$

This follows from the fact that at equilibrium, $E_{internal}=0$. As an external field is applied, the chargest move / seperate, creating an E field within the conductor.

Given that the E at any point within the conductor is $E_{external}+E_{internal}$, then charges continue to move until an internal E field is created that is exactly equal to an opposite the external field, at which charges cease to move. 

---
All conductors are equipotential. 

Since the E field within the conductor is 0, and V is related to the work required to move an electron, ie defined as so:

$\Delta V = -\displaystyle \int^b_{a} \vec{E} \cdot \overline{dl}$, then voltage change across any two points is 0.

Thus the conductor is equipotential.
